01 · Conceptualisation

Connecting digital experiences with physical places

The idea behind Urban Echo is to explore how digital experiences can be connected to physical places. I was interested in the idea that locations can hold different layers of meaning, information, or experiences that are not always obvious to the people physically present. Urban Echo aims to use location-aware technology, such as GPS on a mobile device to recognise where a user is and respond by showing content that is connected to that physical location.

Urban Echo relates to responsive design by reacting to the context the user is in. In this case, the main input is the user’s physical location. The app would use that location to determine what content is relevant and then change the experience in response.

This makes the experience different from most normal mobile apps where the same information is available regardless of where the user is physically located. With Urban Echo, moving through the physical world directly affects what can be seen and experienced.

A key part of the concept is that the physical place is not just the background for the experience, it becomes part of the interface itself.

From this starting point, the concept can then be explored in a number of different ways depending on what kind of content is connected to each location and who is able to contribute to it.

02 · Concept Realisations

Exploring how the concept could be used

After developing the initial idea for Urban Echo, I started thinking about the different ways the concept could actually be used. The core idea stayed the same, but the purpose of that content changed as the concept developed.

01

Realisation 1

Landmark Stories

This realisation was based around significant landmarks, monuments and historical places. The idea was a mobile web application in which a user could physically visit a location and once within a certain GPS range, the app would reveal digital content connected to that place. This could include historical photos, videos, audio recordings, written information, archival materials or stories about events that happened there.

02

Realisation 2 · Current Direction

User-Created “Echoes”

This idea builds on the original realisation however, instead of displaying generic, already available information on historical landmarks, it takes a more personal approach. Users are able to create and upload their own media to any physical location within the app. This would include physical locations that otherwise people may think have not much significance to them, but to others holds really special memories.

⌖

Example Echo

A couple could upload photos and a story attached to the restaurant where they got engaged. Other users visiting that restaurant could discover their Echo and also create their own to share an experience they had at the same place.

An Echo could include things such as photos, videos, audio recordings, written memories or other media. Over time, locations could build up a collection of different experiences from the people who have visited them, creating a digital layer of memories connected to the physical world.

This is the realisation I would like to develop further, as I think it moves Urban Echo beyond simply providing location-based information and turns users into active contributors to the experience.

03 · Initial App Function & UI

A live map that responds to where the user is

Initial visual direction for the mobile web application.

At this stage, I imagine Urban Echo working as a mobile web application built around a live map interface that responds to the user’s current location.

When the user opens the app, they would first be asked for permission to access their location. Once this is enabled, the live map would centre on the user’s current position and display nearby locations that contain Echoes.

The interface could include

  • 01A live map showing the user’s current location
  • 02Markers showing nearby Echoes
  • 03A distance indicator showing how far away each Echo is
  • 04A page or pop-up for viewing the media attached to an Echo
  • 05A button for creating a new Echo at the user’s current location
  • 06An upload screen for adding text, photos, audio or video

Proximity as part of the interaction

The app could also respond visually as the user moves closer to an Echo. For example, an Echo marker could become more noticeable as the user approaches it, with the full content becoming available once they are within a certain distance.

This would make the map more than just a navigation tool. The user’s real-world movement would directly influence what they can see and interact with in the application.

04 · Existing Related Projects

Looking at existing location-based experiences

These projects helped identify existing approaches to live maps, location-aware interaction and user-generated content, while also highlighting where Urban Echo could differ.

Related Project 01

Snapchat’s Snap Map Feature

The social media platform Snapchat has a “Snap Map” feature that allows users to upload photos and videos that then appear on a location-based map for other users to view. Snapchat also includes a “Places” feature where users can explore real-world locations such as restaurants, shops and landmarks and view community content connected to those places.

This is relevant to Urban Echo as it shows how user-generated media can be linked to physical locations through a live map interface. It demonstrates that people are already familiar with the idea of exploring digital content based on where it was created or submitted.

Urban Echo differs from these features in a few important ways. Snapchat’s content is generally temporary and is mainly restricted to photos and videos, while Urban Echo would allow users to upload a wider range of content such as written memories, audio recordings, photos and videos. Snapchat users also do not have to physically be at a location to view the content connected to it, as they can browse different places remotely through the map. Urban Echo would instead make physical presence an important part of the experience, with users needing to be within a certain range of an Echo before they are able to view it. This would encourage users to physically visit locations and discover memories or experiences that have been left there by other people.

Related Project 02

Pokémon GO

Pokémon Go is a well-known location-based mobile game that uses the player’s real-world location as part of the gameplay experience. The main interface is a live map that reflects the player’s current position and displays nearby Pokemon, PokeStops, and Gyms that are connected to real-world locations. Players need to physically move through their environment and get within a certain range before they interact with the game features.

This is relevant to Urban Echo because it demonstrates how a live map can respond to the user’s current location and encourage them to physically move through real-world spaces to access digital content. The interaction radius used in Pokémon GO is particularly relevant, as Urban Echo could use a similar approach where users need to be within a certain distance of an Echo before they are able to view or interact with it.

Urban Echo would differ from Pokémon GO in its purpose and the type of content being discovered. Pokémon GO mainly uses location and movement as part of a game, with players travelling to locations to collect items, find Pokémon or interact with Gyms. Urban Echo would instead use location-based interaction to reveal memories, stories and media connected to real places. Users would also be able to contribute their own Echoes, meaning the content would be created from people’s experiences rather than being focused around game objectives.

05 · Task 3 — Prototype Development

Turning Urban Echo into something people can use

19–22 October 2026

For Task 3, I wanted to turn the idea from Task 2 into a working mobile experience. My focus was on discovering a memory by being near its location, then being able to leave something there for someone else.

The app developed through a series of smaller changes. I kept returning to the mock-up, trying the interface on my phone and asking for changes when something felt crowded or did not work as expected. This section follows those decisions, including the parts that still need testing.

I used AI assistance for coding, debugging and automated checks. The design direction and revisions came from my concept, references and feedback. Screenshot captions identify browser tests that use simulated GPS or test media; these are separate from observations made on my phone.

19 October · Getting the core working

Making location part of the experience

I wanted Task 3 to take Urban Echo beyond the mock-up. The main idea was still the same: a place holds a memory, and being there is what lets someone discover it. The first priority was getting that relationship working with actual browser location data.

The first version combined a map, nearby memories and a form that saves an Echo at the current GPS position. I started with device-only storage so the basic create, save and discover loop could be developed before adding public sharing. The app was then moved into its own repository, keeping this journal separate from the working prototype.

I chose 100 metres as a starting radius. GPS is not exact, so the app also checks the accuracy of each reading instead of treating the map pin as a perfect position. This can make discovery more cautious near the edge of the radius. I still need an outdoor walk to judge whether the distance and 30-second location expiry feel right.

19 October · Photos and shared memories

Making Echoes available to other people

A memory app needs more than text. I wanted people to be able to attach a photo and find memories left by someone else, while keeping the option to save something only on their own device.

The photo flow added a preview, resizing and a delete action. Shared Echoes were then connected to Supabase so a contribution could be loaded in a separate browser session. Public and device-only storage became an explicit choice in the creation form.

I kept contributing simple by using an anonymous browser identity instead of a sign-up screen. The tradeoff is that clearing browser data loses access to that identity. Public Echoes also share their content and exact pin: the nearby-unlock interaction should not be mistaken for private storage.

21 October · Mobile layout

Giving the map more room

The app needed quite a bit of work on mobile. Too much information was squeezed into the map screen, and the nearby list was competing with the map rather than helping it.

I asked for the map to take up more of the screen, a closer default zoom and smaller location controls. Nearby and My Echoes became separate browsing views. I also removed the top media filters because they added clutter without doing much for the main experience.

After removing those controls, I still wanted the map to give people a reason to explore. The small discovery card now shows whether any Echoes are within reach and opens the nearby list. It gives the screen a clear next action while leaving most of the map visible.

21 October · Audio, video and mixed attachments

Keeping different parts of a memory together

I wanted Echoes to include sound and movement as well as photos. Recording audio or video made sense for the idea: a voice, a short moment or the sound of a place can add something that a written description cannot.

The first media version allowed one attachment. I then asked for all media types to belong to the same Echo, rather than making people choose between a photo, audio and video. The creation form now supports a collection, with a preview and separate remove action for each item.

The collection is limited to eight attachments and 50 MB in total. Those are prototype limits to keep capture and uploads manageable, rather than thresholds proven through user testing. File selection remains available when recording permissions or browser support get in the way.

21 October · Revisiting the detail screen

Letting the story lead

I wanted the viewing screen to feel closer to the original mock-up. An initial revision put a large image above the story and used thumbnails to switch between the other visuals. It looked more grouped, but the media was still getting ahead of the meaning of the Echo.

I changed the order again: title and description first, then audio, followed by the full photos and videos. I preferred ordinary scrolling to hiding the other attachments behind thumbnail selection.

The audio player also needed to feel like part of Urban Echo. I supplied a reference for a large play button and waveform. The finished control includes play/pause, seeking and elapsed time, with a waveform taken from the recording rather than a decorative pattern. The same control was then added to the creation preview.

21 October · Likes, replies and Activity

Giving people a way to respond

Once shared memories were working, I wanted visitors to be able to like an Echo or leave a reply. That adds a small social connection around the place, rather than making discovery a one-way interaction.

Likes can be toggled and replies can be deleted by their author. These controls appear when a public Echo is unlocked nearby. The bottom Journal link was replaced with Activity, which shows likes and replies received on the current browser owner’s public Echoes.

Anonymous identity keeps this simple, but it is also a limitation. Activity belongs to the browser identity rather than a recoverable account. Moderation and account recovery would need more attention before treating the prototype as a wider public service.

22 October · Testing on my phone

Fixing what the browser checks missed

My iPhone screenshots highlighted issues that the automated checks had not caught. One Echo title appeared as a tall vertical strip over the map, so I asked for the floating title to be removed. Titles are still available in the list and memory view.

The video preview was another example. Although a frame was generated in the browser tests, my phone still showed a blank video area. The latest revision uses an image cover and its own Play button instead of relying only on the native video poster. I still need to confirm that change on the iPhone.

I also wanted time to prepare before recording. Opening video now shows the camera for framing, and opening audio confirms the microphone is ready. A separate Start recording action begins the clip and timer. Cancel closes the preview without creating an attachment.

22 October · Existing video files

Checking the other way to add a video

Choosing an existing video was not working as expected, so this needed a separate check from recording a new clip. The file importer now handles missing or unusual type information and waits for readable duration metadata.

The limits appear beside the chooser, and rejection messages give the file size or duration when relevant. Valid selections lead into the preview, while a rejected file leaves the existing draft intact.

Generated MP4 and MOV files passed the import, preview and playback checks. An imported MP4 was also shared and played in another browser session. I still need to repeat the original phone selection; these checks do not establish what happened to that particular file.

22 October · Proximity as interaction

Noticing a memory as I move closer

I wanted the user's movement to affect the map before the memory opens. Echo pins now become larger and more visible, with a soft halo, as someone approaches from 500 metres away.

A nearby Echo with uncertain GPS stays amber. The strongest teal ring appears when the existing 100-metre, accuracy-aware rule unlocks the memory. Without a fresh location, pins return to a neutral state.

The aim is to make the map feel more responsive to being there, while keeping the actual content reveal distinct. The browser checks passed, including reduced motion and tap targets; I still need to judge the approach distance during an outdoor phone test.

Where it stands

What is working, and what I still need to do

The prototype now has the main loop I wanted: find a place, unlock a memory, and create an Echo using text, photos, audio or video. Shared storage and Activity give people a way to contribute and respond.

The latest location checks passed 36 code-level checks and 22 browser checks. The earlier file-import checks passed 19 local media and 15 shared-media checks. Those results help catch regressions, but the blank video on my iPhone showed why they cannot replace testing on the actual device.

My next priorities are to confirm the latest phone fixes, walk outside and back into an Echo’s radius, and observe how a few other people use the app. I also need to record the standalone showreel and finish my final evaluation using those results.

The biggest design lesson so far is that adding features does not automatically make the experience better. Much of the useful progress came from removing clutter, changing the order of information and making actions more deliberate.
